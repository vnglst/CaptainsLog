## Raw Script

So I want to spend a bit of time on something that's been at the back of my mind for a few weeks, which is the question of where I'm going and whether I'm being intentional about it. This is going to be a mixture of mentoring reflections and career direction thoughts because in my head they're connected right now.

Let me start with Wesley Crusher because he's the proximate thing that's making me think about this. I've been mentoring Wesley somewhat informally for about two months. When he joined Starfleet Analytics Will Riker suggested that I be his main point of contact for technical questions, and that's grown into a more regular thing. We pair program for about ninety minutes on Tuesday and Thursday mornings. And I want to say, the pair programming sessions have been good. Wesley is fast, he picks things up quickly, and he has good instincts about when to stop and question an assumption versus when to just try something and see what happens. In the pair sessions he's excellent.

The challenge is what happens when he works solo. He has a strong tendency to go deep into a rabbit hole when he hits something he doesn't understand, and he'll spend two or three hours researching a problem before surfacing to ask for help. That's not always wrong behavior. Sometimes deep investigation is the right call. But there's a calibration issue. He'll spend two hours trying to understand the theoretical underpinning of something before he's written a single line of code, and by the time he surfaces he's confused and slightly overwhelmed and the original task has ballooned in his head. What he needs is more frequent check-ins with a shorter feedback loop, and I haven't been providing that. I've been relying on him to surface when he needs help and he doesn't do that until he's already in quite deep.

I mentioned this to Guinan over coffee last week. She asked me to describe what I was trying to accomplish when I mentor Wesley. I gave her an answer about technical skills and process habits. She asked again, more specifically: what do I want him to be able to do that he can't do now, and what would that look like in practice? And then she said something that I keep turning over. She said: "The risk in mentoring is you teach the solution instead of teaching the thinking." And she's right. When we pair program I tend to narrate my own thought process and Wesley follows it, and he learns the solution for that problem. But he doesn't necessarily learn the meta-skill, which is: when you're stuck, what do you do? What's the sequence of steps you go through? How do you decide when to dig deeper versus when to ask? I've been teaching the answer when I should be teaching the method.

Since that conversation I've changed how I run the pair sessions slightly. Instead of narrating what I'm doing, I ask Wesley to narrate what he thinks we should do next, and then I ask questions about his reasoning. It's slower. He finds it slightly frustrating because he can see I know the answer and he has to sit with the uncertainty longer. But I think it's better. I'm going to keep it up.

The second thing I want to talk about is the Deanna Troi leadership workshop. This was a two-day workshop that ran last month. Deanna runs these periodically for anyone at Starfleet Analytics who's at a certain seniority level or moving toward a more senior role. The framing was around psychological safety, specifically how team leads and senior engineers create or destroy it without necessarily intending to. The core model was straightforward: people need to feel safe to speak up, to be wrong, to surface problems early. But the mechanism through which psychological safety is created turned out to be more nuanced than I expected. A lot of it is about what leaders do when things go wrong. How you respond in the first thirty seconds after someone tells you about a problem sets the tone for how likely they are to tell you about the next problem. I've been thinking about how I responded after the authentication regression. I think I did it reasonably well — I tried to focus on the process and not on Wesley personally. But I want to be more conscious of it.

There was a specific exercise in the workshop where you had to recall a time when you had not spoken up about something you'd observed in a professional setting, and think about what had made you stay silent. I thought of a situation from about eighteen months ago where I noticed that a piece of the Replicator API design was going in a direction I didn't agree with and I said nothing because I wasn't sure it was my place to push back and I didn't want to slow things down. That design decision ended up causing rework about five months later that I helped with. I didn't share this in the group exercise but I wrote it down. The workshop was good. Deanna is a good facilitator. She creates exactly the kind of environment she's teaching about.

The career direction piece. Q from Q Ventures reached out to me last week. Direct message saying they're expanding their portfolio analytics function and they're looking for senior engineers who want to help build it. It was flattering. Q Ventures is well funded and the problem domain is interesting. I am not interested. It's not the right time, it's not the right thing, and I've heard enough stories about Q's approach to leadership to be quite confident about the not right thing part. But it was flattering.

What the message did make me do was think more seriously about the fork I've been avoiding. Staff engineer track or management track. I've been treating this as a question for future me to answer and future me is running out of road to hand it back to. My current read: I'm more energized by the technical depth than by the people management work. I enjoy the mentoring, but when I try to imagine a role that's ninety percent people and process and ten percent technical, that sounds depleting rather than energizing. Whereas a staff engineer role that's still deeply technical but has organizational impact, that sounds right.

I've been reading "The Staff Engineer's Path" by Tanya Reilly. Data Chen recommended it six months ago and I finally started it. It's the clearest articulation of what the staff track actually involves that I've read. The thing that's resonating most is the framing around the technical direction problem. A staff engineer's job is not to be the best individual contributor but to identify the leverage points where technical decisions have outsized organizational impact and put their energy there. That's not how I've been thinking about my own work. I've been thinking about it task by task. I need to think about it at a higher level of abstraction. This book is making me consider having a more direct conversation with Will Riker about what the path forward looks like in the context of Starfleet Analytics specifically.

That's the main thread. Mentoring, psychological safety, career direction, all running together in my head this week.
