# TNG-01: An Engineer at El-Adrel

**Language:** English
**Recording target:** ~10 minutes (~1,185 words at a relaxed speaking pace)
**Categories:** work, personal, side-project
**Audio filename:** `tng-01-work-week-review.m4a`
**Recording status:** To be recorded by the owner.
**Episode:** Darmok (TNG, season 5, episode 2). An invented engineer’s perspective on the televised encounter; private conversations and off-duty reflections are fictional.

## Raw Script

Personal log. My shift in engineering is over, but I can still hear the transporter status tones when I close my eyes. We have left the Tamarian ship behind. Captain Jean-Luc Picard is back aboard the Enterprise. Dathon is dead. Those three facts fit into a few seconds of speech, and somehow they still do not fit together in my head. I was there for the encounter at El-Adrel, watching readings and waiting for orders. I want to remember what it felt like from our side of the console, before knowing the ending made everything seem obvious.

When we first heard the Tamarians, I assumed we had a translator fault. The sounds became familiar words, but the sentences did not tell me what anyone wanted. I checked the diagnostic display twice. Nothing indicated a failure. Geordi La Forge reminded me that a successful diagnostic only answers the question it was designed to answer. It showed that the equipment was operating. It did not show that two people understood each other. That distinction sounds simple now. At the time I kept looking for a damaged component, because a damaged component would have given me something useful to do.

Then Picard and Dathon were transported to the surface. I was at an engineering station when the situation changed. I remember reaching for the next procedure before anyone had finished explaining what had happened. My assignment was to help check the transporter systems and record the interference affecting our attempts to retrieve the captain. I did not beam anyone anywhere. I was not on the surface. Most of what I knew came from status messages, the instruments in front of me, and the voices of people who had more information than I did.

The Tamarian scattering field made the problem feel deliberately cruel. We knew where the captain was, and the ship had transporters, yet that did not mean we could bring him home. I checked our internal systems with Geordi and compared the results against the interference readings. I wanted to find an ordinary fault on our side, something we could isolate and replace. The checks did not give us that answer. I wrote down what they actually showed. I did not write that the transporters had been repaired, because I had not found a broken transporter to repair.

Commander William Riker was responsible for decisions that reached far beyond my station. From engineering I heard requests and responses, not every discussion on the bridge. I could feel the urgency in the short pauses between messages. There were moments when I thought another attempt must work simply because we needed it to work. That is an uncomfortable thing to admit. A warning indicator does not change its meaning because the person watching it is frightened. I kept reading the values aloud to myself, slowly, so that I would report what was there rather than what I hoped to see.

Meanwhile Data and Deanna Troi were working on the language problem. The explanation that eventually reached us was stranger than a defective translator. The Tamarians were communicating through references to stories their listeners were expected to know. We could recognize words without knowing the experiences those words called up. I thought about maintenance shorthand. Two engineers can mention an old incident and immediately understand why a particular check matters. Someone arriving for their first shift hears the same words and learns almost nothing. That comparison helped me, although a whole language built around shared stories is far more than a few workshop jokes.

I learned afterwards that Picard and Dathon had faced danger together on the surface. I did not see their camp or hear their whole conversation. I am being careful about that because it would be easy to tell this log as though I had stood beside them. What I witnessed was the waiting aboard the Enterprise, the blocked rescue attempts, and the uncertainty about what the other ship intended. What I learned later was that Dathon had been trying to make contact through an experience both captains could share. By then he had paid a price none of our diagnostic displays could express.

When Picard finally communicated with the Tamarian ship, the change was not a new piece of hardware coming online. He had gained enough understanding of their stories to make himself understood. The confrontation ended. I remember realizing that I was holding my shoulders stiffly and had been doing so for hours. There was relief at hearing the captain again, followed almost immediately by the knowledge that Dathon had died. I had spent the encounter thinking about getting one man back. It took a little longer to think properly about the man who would never return to his own ship.

After duty I went to Ten Forward. Guinan was there, and I ordered tea mostly because it gave my hands something to do. I did not feel like explaining the entire shift. I said that we had translated the words and still missed the meaning. Saying it aloud made the failure feel less like an abstract puzzle. I watched other people talking around the room. So much of an ordinary conversation depends on what people already know about one another. A familiar name, a remembered place, a joke from yesterday. I wondered how much I leave unexplained without even noticing.

Back in my quarters I opened a text about Gilgamesh, the story Picard had shared with Dathon. I was not looking for a technical solution. I wanted to spend a little time with something that had helped one person reach another. I stopped reading after a few pages and sat quietly. Tomorrow there will be routine work, and the ship will need the same careful attention it needed before this encounter. Tonight I do not want to rush past the fact that understanding arrived too late to save Dathon, even though it prevented further violence between our crews.

I have started drafting my engineering incident report for Starfleet. The diagnostic records are saved, but the report has not been submitted. I still need to check the order of the transporter attempts against the duty log. My separate evening project, the Metaphor Notebook, has one new entry about the encounter. That is a personal collection of reflections, not an official account of Tamarian language. I want the distinction clear. I can describe what I observed and what I was told afterwards. I cannot turn a few difficult hours into complete knowledge of another people.

For the next watch, my plan is ordinary: finish checking the timestamps, ask Geordi to review the technical section, and submit the report only after those checks. I have not changed the transporter hardware or claimed a solution to the language problem. Picard is aboard. The confrontation is over. Dathon is gone. I keep returning to those facts because each matters, and none cancels out the others. I am an engineer, and I still want clear readings and working equipment. After El-Adrel, I also want to become better at noticing when clear readings are answering only part of the question.
