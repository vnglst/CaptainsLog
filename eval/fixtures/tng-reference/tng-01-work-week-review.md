## Raw Script

Personal log. We're still on course for the survey station, and for once I've finished my shift before the lights in the corridor change to the night setting. I thought I'd record this while I can still remember the order things happened in. Most of the week has been spent in engineering, apart from a few hours on the bridge and one afternoon outside the ship. That last part sounds more exciting than it felt at the time. Mostly I was trying not to drop a tool.

On our first morning out from the last starbase, Geordi La Forge asked me to look at a fluctuation in the starboard warp nacelle. Nothing serious enough to take us out of warp. Just a small difference between the commanded power and the reading coming back from one of the sensors. It only appeared above warp six. Below that, everything looked normal. We could have left it until the next scheduled inspection, but Geordi wanted an explanation before we got too far from somewhere we could stop for repairs.

I thought we had a sensor drifting out of calibration. Geordi wasn't convinced. He'd seen the same reading twice, at almost exactly the same point in the power cycle. We spent the first hour comparing the nacelle telemetry with the readings from main engineering. Then we repeated the test at a lower speed. Nothing. Captain Jean-Luc Picard agreed to hold us at warp five while we investigated. I was grateful for that. It's easier to think when you know nobody is about to ask the engines for more power.

Data came down after his bridge watch. He found that the sensor itself was fine. The maintenance profile loaded into the monitoring system belonged to the assembly we'd replaced at the starbase. The new assembly needed a different compensation setting. So the fluctuation on our display wasn't a real power fluctuation at all. We corrected the profile and ran the test again. This time the readings agreed. Geordi still kept us at warp five until we'd completed a second check. We were back at cruising speed before the evening shift.

I've added the profile check to the nacelle inspection instructions. Wesley Crusher helped me go through the records for the other sensors, and we found one more old entry. That one hadn't affected anything yet. We corrected it anyway. I want him to understand why we checked the whole set instead of stopping when the first warning disappeared. It's tempting to call a job finished as soon as the display turns green. I've done it myself. This was a useful reminder to look at what the display is actually telling us.

The next day we launched a probe toward a small nebula off our route. The science team wanted readings from a region the Enterprise couldn't safely enter. I was on the bridge to watch the probe's power system during launch. From the aft station, the nebula looked almost solid, like a wall of pale blue cloud. You can forget how empty most of it is when you're looking at it on a screen. The probe disappeared from view in seconds, but its signal kept coming back clearly.

About twenty minutes later, the signal began to break up. Worf checked for interference from another vessel. There was no sign of one. Data thought the local radiation was disrupting the transmission, so we adjusted the relay frequency. That helped, but we still lost a short section of the readings. The probe is continuing its survey. We haven't recovered the missing section, and I don't want my notes to suggest that we have. The science team says the rest is useful. We'll know more when the full transmission arrives.

My afternoon outside the ship came the following day. One of the small sensor covers near the port shuttle bay hadn't seated properly after an earlier repair. A maintenance drone couldn't reach the catch from the right angle, so Geordi sent two of us out in suits. I'd checked the suit seals three times before we reached the airlock. I know the procedure. That doesn't stop me wanting to check once more when the other side of the door is open space.

The repair itself was straightforward. My tether kept drifting across the place where I needed to work, which made everything slower. My partner held it clear while I reset the catch. We checked the cover, checked the surrounding hull, and went back inside. No emergency, no last-second rescue. Just a very ordinary job in a place where an ordinary mistake would matter a lot. When I got back to my quarters, I stood under the sonic shower for longer than necessary. I hadn't realised how tense my shoulders were.

Commander William Riker asked me afterwards whether Wesley could observe the next external inspection from the control station. I said yes, provided we have time to talk through it first. Watching suit telemetry is useful, but I want him to know which changes need an immediate response and which ones are normal. Riker also asked for a short briefing on the nacelle issue before we reach the survey station. I'll put that together tomorrow. It doesn't need to be long, just clear about what happened and what we changed.

There was also a small problem with the replicators on deck twelve. Several people reported that hot drinks were coming out lukewarm. Food was fine. The maintenance team traced it to a temperature setting shared by a group of beverage patterns. We corrected it, then asked the people who had reported it to try again. One crew member said the tea was now too hot. I don't think we'll ever get everyone to agree about tea, but at least the replicators are producing what the selected pattern actually specifies.

I went to Ten Forward after that shift. Guinan was there, and Geordi joined me for a while. We started talking about the nacelle warning and ended up talking about how difficult it is to admit you've spent an hour following the wrong idea. Geordi said the hard part is noticing when you've stopped testing a theory and started defending it. That stayed with me. When Data arrived with the maintenance records, I was still trying to prove the sensor was faulty. I could have listened sooner.

I haven't had much time on the holodeck this week. I'd reserved an hour for a walk through a coastal landscape, but I gave the slot to someone whose family had just come aboard at the last starbase. I'll find another time. For now, even a few minutes in the observation lounge helps. Watching the stars without a diagnostic display in front of them feels different. I spend so much time keeping this ship moving that I sometimes forget to look at where we're going.

Tomorrow I need to finish the briefing for Riker, check the next transmission from the probe, and go through the external inspection procedure with Wesley. The nacelle readings are stable, and the sensor cover is secure. The missing probe data is still missing. That's where things stand. I'm going to get something to eat, then try to sleep before the early watch. If nothing unexpected happens overnight, we should reach the survey station on schedule. I'd quite like a week where that sentence turns out to be true.
