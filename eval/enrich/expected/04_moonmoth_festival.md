I spent the evening at the Moonmoth Feast in Tinkermere. Everything in this diary belongs to an invented world: the town, the people, the creatures and the festival. Nera Pindle met me at Brindle Quay with a basket of folded paper lanterns. Fenn Tallow arrived later, carrying a wooden puppet shaped like a sleepy fish. Neither of them had remembered the ribbon for the puppet's tail, so we tied on a strip of blue cloth instead. The fish looked much happier after that. I wanted to remember the small details of the evening before they became confused with the stories everyone told on the way home.

The first lantern we opened had a crooked star on its side. Nera said the crooked ones were easier to recognize when they floated past the quay. We were not allowed to release them into the water. Each lantern hung from a cord above the path, and someone would take it down at the end of the feast. The light came from a small glowstone in a clay cup. Fenn checked the cups before lifting the cords, because a loose cup could fall onto somebody's hat. We found one cracked cup and exchanged it for a sound one. Nothing dramatic happened, but it was reassuring to see somebody notice the crack before the path became crowded.

The moonmoths appeared just after the second bell. They were pale, round creatures with soft wings and tiny green feet. They settled on the lantern cords rather than on the lanterns themselves. One sat on Fenn's sleeve for so long that he gave up trying to move his arm. He did not give the creature a name. I mention that because he later joked that half the feast had been spent waiting for his sleeve to become available again. Nera counted seven moths along the nearest cord, then stopped counting when more arrived from the reeds. There was no competition and nobody was collecting them. We simply watched them arrive.

At the fork in the path, the sign for Velvet Reach pointed towards the water instead of towards the footbridge. We turned it around. A few visitors had already followed the wrong arrow and were standing beside an empty landing stage, wondering whether a boat was coming. Fenn walked over to explain that the footbridge was still open. Nera stayed at the fork until everyone had found the right path. I carried the lantern basket across first, then came back for the puppet. The bridge swayed a little in the middle, but the handrail was firm and the boards were dry. We waited for the people coming the other way before crossing again.

On the far bank, children were making shadows with folded leaves. A broad leaf became a bird, a narrow one became a fish, and a torn leaf became something nobody could agree about. Fenn used the wooden fish puppet to tell a story about a creature that wanted to sleep through the feast. Every time the fish closed its eyes, another bell rang. The children supplied increasingly ridiculous excuses for the bells. There was no script and no prize for the best excuse. When a smaller child could not see, Nera moved the lantern higher and everyone shuffled backwards. That was enough to give the child a clear view.

We had brought more paper lanterns than we needed. Rather than hang them all in one place, we kept the unopened ones in the basket. A gust of wind later tore two lanterns near Brindle Quay, and the spare ones were useful then. We collected the torn paper immediately so it would not drift into the reeds. One of the glowstones had rolled beneath a bench. I found it by following the green patch of light on the underside of the seat. Its clay cup was intact. Fenn put the cup back in the basket and used a spare lantern frame. The repaired stretch of path looked slightly uneven, which I liked.

The feast ended with a quiet walk back from Velvet Reach. There was no final speech. People carried their cups and folded leaves with them, and the puppet travelled on top of the empty basket. Nera took down the lanterns while Fenn untied the cords. I checked the path and the landing stage for scraps of paper. We found the missing blue ribbon caught on the bench, although by then the cloth tail had become part of the fish's character. Fenn kept the cloth on the puppet and folded the ribbon away for another evening.

Back at Brindle Quay, we counted the glowstones into their box. One was missing until Nera remembered the lantern still hanging over the fork in the path. We fetched it together. The arrow remained pointed towards the footbridge, and all the cups were back in the basket. I felt pleasantly tired. The best part of the Moonmoth Feast was watching people make room for one another without turning it into a grand occasion. Tomorrow I want to draw the fish puppet and the moth on Fenn's sleeve. Tonight I am leaving the basket by the door and keeping this diary as my reminder of a small, peculiar celebration in Tinkermere.
